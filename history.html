The Commodore 64 story

Two chips designed to sell to someone else, a boss who wanted a computer for the masses, and the best-selling single computer model there has ever been. It is the machine READY-Prompt emulates, down to the cycle.

1The $25 processor

In 1975 a microprocessor cost hundreds of dollars. Chuck Peddle and a group of engineers left Motorola, where they had worked on the 6800, for MOS Technology, a chip maker in Pennsylvania, and designed a simpler, cheaper processor. MOS sold the 6502 from a hotel suite at the WESCON show in September 1975, for $25.

It had one accumulator, two index registers and a stack of 256 bytes, and it made up for that with fast access to the first 256 bytes of memory, the zero page, and clever addressing. The Apple II, the Atari 400 and 800, the BBC Micro and the NES were all built on it. So, in a version with an I/O port of its own, the 6510, was the C64.

The 6510's port

  • Addresses 0 and 1 are not memory but a little I/O port inside the CPU.
  • Its bits 0-2 tell the PLA what to show: BASIC, the KERNAL, the character ROM, I/O, or RAM underneath.
  • Bits 3-5 drive the Datasette: the data out, the PLAY sense and the motor.

2Jack Tramiel's Commodore

Jack Tramiel (1928–2012) survived Auschwitz, emigrated to the United States, learned to repair typewriters in the US Army and started a typewriter repair business in New York, which became Commodore Business Machines in Toronto in 1958. Commodore made typewriters, then adding machines, then calculators; when Texas Instruments started making calculators of its own and selling them below Commodore's prices, Tramiel decided never again to depend on someone else's chips. In 1976 Commodore bought MOS Technology, and with it the 6502 and Chuck Peddle.

Peddle built the PET 2001 (1977), one of the first computers sold assembled, with its monitor and a cassette deck in one case. The VIC-20 (1980 in Japan, 1981 elsewhere) was cheap and colourful, advertised by William Shatner, and became the first computer to sell a million. Tramiel's slogan was "computers for the masses, not the classes". His other one, as the C64's engineers remembered it, was that business is war.

3Two chips in search of a computer

In 1981 the engineers at MOS designed two chips meant for video games and arcade machines: a graphics chip, the VIC-II (Al Charpentier), and a sound chip, the SID (Bob Yannes). In November 1981 Tramiel agreed to build a computer around them, with 64 kilobytes of RAM, when 64K was a lot. The team had a prototype running for the Consumer Electronics Show in Las Vegas in January 1982, about two months later.

The C64 went on sale in August 1982 at $595. Its BASIC was BASIC V2, Microsoft's 6502 BASIC, which Commodore had licensed for the PET for a one-off fee, reportedly $25,000. It had no commands for the graphics or the sound: those were PEEKs and POKEs into the chips' registers, and programmers learned the addresses by heart: 53280 for the border, 53281 for the background, 54272 for the SID.

A cut-down sibling, the Commodore MAX Machine (or Ultimax), was sold in Japan in 1982 as a games console with 2K of RAM, running its cartridges with no KERNAL at all. It failed, but every C64 still has its memory map: a cartridge that pulls the GAME line low puts the C64 in Ultimax mode, and EasyFlash cartridges boot that way to this day.

READY. measured

  • 38911 BASIC BYTES FREE is 0801h to 9FFFh: 40,960 bytes less the first 2K, less one.
  • The C64C's single ROM chip, 251913-01, holds BASIC and the KERNAL; its second half is, byte for byte, KERNAL 901227-03.
  • A PAL C64 runs at 985,248 Hz, an NTSC one at 1,022,727 Hz: the VIC-II's crystal divided by 18 and by 14.

4The VIC-II

The VIC-II draws 320 × 200 pixels (or 160 × 200 in four colours per cell) in a border of a 16-colour palette, and 8 sprites of 24 × 21 pixels that move anywhere, over or under the text, and report when they touch each other or the background. It can interrupt the CPU on any raster line, so a program can change colours, modes or sprites halfway down the screen and show more than the chip was designed to: more sprites by reusing them, raster bars, split screens, and, by tricking the border logic, pictures in the border itself.

It shares the bus with the CPU. On every eighth line of the text area (a bad line) it needs 40 more cycles to fetch the next row of characters, and it stops the CPU to get them; each sprite on a line takes a couple of cycles more. Demo coders counted these cycles, and so does READY-Prompt.

5The SID

Bob Yannes, who had played with synthesizers, wanted the SID to be one. It has three voices, each with four waveforms (triangle, sawtooth, pulse of any width, and noise), its own envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release), ring modulation and hard sync, and a filter, low-pass, band-pass or high-pass, with resonance, through which any voice can be sent.

The filter was analogue and no two chips sounded quite alike. Changing the volume register also moved a DC level on the original 6581, which let programmers play sampled sound through it (the speech in Ghostbusters, the drums of many late tunes); the 8580 of the C64C fixed that, and so mostly lost the trick. Composers such as Rob Hubbard, Martin Galway and Ben Daglish made it famous, and SID music is still written and played.

The numbers

  • Pitch = register × clock ÷ 224: 7493 is A, 440 Hz, on a PAL machine.
  • The fastest attack is 255 steps of 9 cycles, about 2 ms.
  • The envelope's rate counter compares for equality: lower the rate mid-count and it wraps all the way round, the "ADSR bug" musicians learned to live with.
  • Voice 3's output can be read at 54299; with noise it is the random number generator of many games.

6The price war

Commodore made its own chips, so it could cut prices further than anyone. In 1983 it cut them again and again, and offered a rebate for trading in any computer, or games console, for a C64. Texas Instruments, which had started the calculator war years before, lost hundreds of millions on its TI-99/4A and left the home computer business in October 1983. By then the C64 sold for around $200, in toy shops and department stores as well as computer shops.

In January 1984, after a dispute with Commodore's chairman, Irving Gould, Tramiel left the company he had founded. Within months he had bought Atari's computer division, and several of Commodore's engineers went with him.

7Tape, disk and patience

Programs came on cassette, played on the Datasette, a tape recorder that only Commodore's machines could use: the computer itself measures the time between pulses on the tape. The KERNAL's own format writes every block twice, so a program loaded at about 50 bytes a second. Games came with their own turbo loaders, ten times faster, often with a picture and music while the tape played.

The 1541 disk drive was a computer of its own, with a 6502, 2K of RAM and its own DOS in ROM, talking to the C64 over a serial bus whose timing was so cautious that it, too, was slow: a few hundred bytes a second. Fast-loader cartridges and programs that reprogrammed the drive's CPU fixed that, and became an industry.

On tape measured

  • A bit is two pulses: short-medium for 0, medium-short for 1 (391, 537 and 700 µs on PAL).
  • A byte is a long-medium marker, 8 bits and a parity bit: about 9,400 cycles.
  • A file is a 10-second leader, the header twice, a 2-second leader, the data twice.

8The scene

Cracked games came with an intro by their crackers, and the intros grew into demos: programs that did nothing but show off, with more colours, sprites and border tricks than anyone thought the chips could manage, and a SID tune. The demo scene that started on the C64 in the mid-1980s is still releasing new C64 productions every year, and its archive, CSDb, lists tens of thousands of them.

9The C64C, and the end

In 1986 the C64 got a slimmer, lighter case like the Commodore 128's, the C64C, and later a cheaper board with fewer chips: BASIC and the KERNAL in one ROM, the 251913-01 whose image READY-Prompt runs, and the 8580 SID. Commodore kept making it until the company went bankrupt in April 1994. How many were sold is disputed: estimates run from about 12.5 to 17 million, which makes it, by the Guinness count, the best-selling single computer model.

10And in Brazil?

As far as we know, the C64 was never made or officially sold in Brazil. From 1984 the Informatics Law reserved the small computer market for Brazilian companies, which built clones of the machines they could copy: Sinclairs (Microdigital's TKs, the subject of READY-Prompt's sibling K-Prompt), MSXs (Gradiente's Expert and Sharp's Hotbit, in Ok-Prompt) and Apple IIs. A C64 in Brazil had come in a suitcase from abroad, with its PAL or NTSC picture to be adapted to the PAL-M television at home.

11How READY-Prompt emulates it

READY-Prompt runs the real ROMs on emulated chips, one bus cycle at a time: every memory access the CPU makes first runs a cycle of the VIC-II, the CIAs and the rest.

The 6510Every instruction makes the same bus accesses as the real chip, dummy reads and writes included, the undocumented opcodes too. Tom Harte's single-step tests check it cycle by cycle.
The PLAWhich chip answers each address comes from the equations read out of a real C64's 82S100 by Jens Schönfeld, Marko Mäkelä and Mark Smith, evaluated as written.
The VIC-IIEach cycle does its fetches (sprite pointers and data, characters, graphics), sets BA for the CPU and draws its 8 pixels with the registers as they are, following Christian Bauer's description of the chip.
The CIAsTimers with the real chip's start, load and interrupt delays; the time of day from the 50 or 60 Hz mains; the keyboard read both ways.
The SIDOscillators, envelopes after reSID, with the ADSR bug, a filter for each model, and the 6581's volume level for samples.
TapesPulses go to CIA1's FLAG line, read by the KERNAL or a turbo loader; KERNAL-format files can also load at once.
The 1541A virtual drive answers the KERNAL's serial routines, so LOAD, SAVE, OPEN and channel 15 behave as with a 1541. Fast loaders that reprogram the drive need a real one.
Cartridges.crt files, with the bank switching of Ocean, Magic Desk, System 3, Dinamic and EasyFlash cartridges.

12The siblings

READY-Prompt is the fourth of a family, each named after the prompt its machine waits at. A-Prompt runs CP/M and its A>; Ok-Prompt is an MSX, which answers Ok; K-Prompt is a ZX Spectrum with its flashing K. All of them dial BBSes through a telnet modem, and can call an AI board (ATDT AI) whose sysop is DeepSeek and which writes programs for the machine that calls it.
